Session 5 · 16.1 The capital structure question

The value of the firm is a pie: V = B + S

A firm owns ₹20,000 of assets. Someone paid for them: shareholders (S) with equity, bondholders (B) with loans. The value of the firm is the sum of the two slices.

Debt B
Equity S

The question of this module: is there a way of slicing the pie that makes the pie itself bigger? If so, management should choose that debt-equity ratio.

Session 5 · 16.2 Firm value or shareholder value?

Shareholders own S, not V. Why should they care about V?

Question 1

Should managers maximise the value of the whole firm, or only the value of the shares?

Question 2

Which debt-equity ratio makes shareholders best off?

Answer

A change in capital structure helps shareholders only if it raises the value of the whole firm. So both goals lead to the same debt-equity ratio.

Session 5 · Modigliani and Miller

The M&M world

Homogeneous expectations

Everyone agrees on the firm's future cash flows.

Same business risk class

The firms compared differ only in how they are financed.

Perpetual cash flows

EBIT is expected to continue forever.

Perfect capital markets

  • Perfect competition
  • Firms and investors borrow and lend at the same rate
  • Everyone has the same information
  • No transaction costs
  • No taxes

Session 5 · Activity · The fundamental insight of M&M

Can an investor copy the levered firm on her own?

The firm stays all equity. Our investor has ₹1,200 of her own. She can also borrow at 8%, as the firm can, and buy shares of the all-equity firm at ₹50. Her target: the levered firm's ROE of 3.0%, 11.3% and 19.7%.

The other way round: homemade unleverage

If the firm borrows but she prefers the all-equity payoff, she buys 24 shares of the levered firm (₹1,200) and ₹800 of its debt. Earnings 36, 136, 236 plus interest 64 give 100, 200, 300 on ₹2,000: 5%, 10%, 15%, the unlevered ROE.

Session 6 · M&M Proposition I (no taxes)

Capital structure does not change firm value

VL = VU
  • Investors can create any amount of leverage on their own account, as the last two slides showed.
  • So the levered firm's shares can only sell at the price that makes both routes cost the same: ₹50.
  • At ₹50, the levered firm is worth 240 × 50 + 8,000 = ₹20,000, the same as the unlevered firm. The firm's choice of debt has not changed its value.

Session 6 · M&M Proposition II (no taxes)

Where does RS = R0 + (B/S)(R0 − RB) come from?

In the expected state the all-equity firm earns 2,000 / 20,000 = 10%. That is R0, the return on unlevered equity. Debt costs RB = 8%.

1 · The firm's return does not change

The assets still earn 10% however they are financed (Proposition I). So the average return to all investors is still R0 = 10%.

2 · Split it between the two groups

(B/V) × RB + (S/V) × RS = R0
(8,000/20,000) × 8% + (12,000/20,000) × RS = 10%

3 · Solve for RS

3.2% + 0.6 RS = 10%
0.6 RS = 6.8%
RS = 11.33%

4 · Spot the formula

RS = 10% + (8,000/12,000)(10% − 8%)
= 10% + (2/3)(2%) = 11.33%
RS = R0 + (B/S)(R0 − RB)

Check: 11.33% is the levered firm's expected ROE from the table, 1,360 / 12,000. Shareholders earn more because they now carry the risk of the debt as well as the business. The book's slide does the same steps in symbols: multiply both sides by (B + S)/S and rearrange.
